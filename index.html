A data story on extreme weather, exposure, and the economics of risk
By Diego Barberena

Storms got safer. Heat got quietly worse.

Eighty-five years of federal weather-death data, matched against U.S. population and migration records, tell two very different stories — and expose a measurement gap that should change how you read the next disaster headline.

The story storms tell

The National Weather Service has tracked tornado, flood, and hurricane deaths since 1940. The raw numbers look like a bumpy, mild improvement. Once you divide by how many Americans were actually alive to be at risk, the improvement looks much larger — and much more real.

Same data, per million Americans

Deaths per million U.S. residents, by decade. The 1950s — the deadliest raw decade — is barely worse than the 1940s once you account for population. The denominator was doing a lot of the "plateau."

The U.S. population nearly tripled between 1940 and 2024. A raw death count that merely holds steady while the population triples is, per person, a huge improvement — better warning systems (Doppler radar, NEXRAD), building codes, and evacuation planning did real work here. Whenever someone shows you a rising or flat disaster-death count, ask what the population — or the number of people actually exposed — was doing at the same time.

Then heat enters the picture

The National Weather Service only started reliably counting heat and cold deaths in 1986. Once you can see them, they look nothing like storms.

What the 1995 spike tells us

The single largest death toll from any weather hazard in this 85-year record, storms included, is 1,021 heat deaths in 1995 — almost entirely the Chicago heat wave. Sociologist Eric Klinenberg's subsequent study found the victims weren't randomly exposed to hot weather: they were disproportionately elderly, poor, and socially isolated — living alone, without air conditioning, afraid to open windows, in neighborhoods with weak social ties where no one checked on them.

Heat was necessary but not sufficient. The death toll was a story of exposure and vulnerability, not just a thermometer reading — the same lesson storms taught us, applied to a hazard with no wind to photograph.

The number nobody agrees on

This is the part to read carefully, because it will come up again with every future disaster statistic you see. There are two entirely different ways to count temperature deaths, and they are not measuring the same thing.

What the NWS counts

The federal count above (≈270/year recently) comes from direct, certified deaths — a coroner attributes a specific death to heat stroke on that day. This is the same method used for tornado and hurricane counts, and it is precise but narrow.

What epidemiologists estimate

Marshall Burke and 15 co-authors synthesize decades of data from 30 countries in "Understanding and Addressing Temperature Impacts on Mortality" (Stanford / NBER Working Paper 34313, October 2025) and estimate temperature is responsible for 5–12% of all deaths — hundreds of thousands per year in the U.S. and EU combined. A separate Yale study (Chu et al., JAMA Network Open, 2025) using U.S. mortality records 2000–2020 estimates roughly 46,000 cold-attributable and 3,400 heat-attributable deaths per year nationally.

Neither estimate is wrong. They use statistical excess-mortality methods — comparing observed deaths on hot or cold days to a modeled "optimal temperature" baseline — which captures heart attacks, respiratory failures, and other indirect deaths that never get coded as "heat death" on paper. The certified-death method and the excess-mortality method are different instruments measuring different things, at completely different scales, and confusing them is one of the easiest mistakes to make when reading disaster statistics. (We saw the same divide earlier with Hurricane Maria: ~7 certified Puerto Rico deaths in NWS-style counts vs. an excess-mortality estimate near 3,000 — same storm, same gap in method.)

Two more findings from Burke et al. worth sitting with: cold kills more than heat in every country they study, and vulnerability shifts with age — the young are more exposed to heat risk, the old to cold. And despite decades of warming, air conditioning, and public-health attention, the overall global burden of temperature-related mortality is not falling over time. That last point lands directly on our chart above: the 2020s heat spike isn't a fluke in the official U.S. data — it matches what the broader epidemiological literature is finding too.

Are we even moving toward the water?

Death rates depend on how many people are exposed, not just how well protected they are. So: are Americans moving toward flood risk, or away from it? Redfin's analysis of Census Bureau domestic migration data (which excludes births and immigration) matched to First Street flood-risk scores, covering the highest-risk 10% of U.S. counties, 2011–2025, gives a genuinely complicated answer.

It depends entirely on the window you look through. Flood-prone America gained residents from 2011 through most of 2019, lost residents 2016–2019 (post-Hurricane Harvey Houston exodus), gained again 2020–2023 (pandemic Sun Belt boom — remote workers moving to Florida and Texas), and is now losing residents again, sharply, in 2024–25 — led by Miami-Dade (−67,418 in 2024 alone), Harris County/Houston, and Brooklyn.

One catch worth flagging in bold: many of these counties still grow in total population even while losing domestic movers — Miami-Dade's population rose 2.3% in 2024 despite the huge domestic outflow, because international immigration (net +123,835) more than filled the gap. Moving to Florida or Texas also doesn't automatically mean moving into a floodplain — "high-risk county" is a coarser unit than an actual flood zone. Exposure is genuinely moving in both directions depending on the year and the geography, which complicates any simple story about whether we're getting more or less at risk.

What economics adds

The raw fatality numbers describe what happened. Economics helps explain why, and what to do next.

Exposure, not hazard, drives the dollar losses too

The IPCC's 2012 Special Report on extreme events (SREX) concluded that increasing exposure of people and economic assets — not a detectable change in storm frequency or intensity — has been the major cause of long-term increases in economic disaster losses, a finding later echoed in the IPCC's 2014 Fifth Assessment Report. This is the dollar-damage analogue of the mortality story above: growth puts more expensive stuff in harm's way, and that alone can make losses trend upward even if storms themselves aren't getting worse.

Yale economist William Nordhaus estimated that about 3% of the U.S. capital stock — roughly $600 billion in structures — sits below 10 meters of elevation in Atlantic hurricane zones. His striking finding: because buildings depreciate and get rebuilt anyway on a fairly short cycle (assume ~50 years), a policy of simply relocating the replacement inland or uphill, rather than rebuilding in place, could cost as little as an estimated 0.01% of GDP per year — a rounding error, because you're not paying to move existing capital, only paying a modest premium on turnover that was happening regardless.

But cheap-in-theory isn't the same as cheap-in-practice. Whether that adaptation actually happens depends on incentives economics is good at diagnosing: subsidized flood insurance (the National Flood Insurance Program has repeatedly rebuilt the same flood-prone properties) can make rebuilding in place artificially cheap; local property-tax bases resist relocating tax-generating structures; and homeowners may simply discount a storm 20 years away too heavily relative to a moving cost today. Wealth and access to technology also shape who survives a given hazard at all — the 1995 Chicago victims lacked air conditioning; today, AC access remains the single strongest predictor of surviving a heat wave, which is itself a story about income and infrastructure, not just weather.

But wait — a good-faith gut check

If I were pushing back on this argument, here's where I'd push, and how it changes the picture.

  • Denominators can mislead in both directions. A national per-capita rate can understate real risk where population is growing fastest specifically in exposed places — the decline we found is a national average, not a guarantee for Miami or Houston.
  • Attributable-mortality estimates are models, not body counts. The Burke/Yale estimates depend on choices about the "minimum mortality temperature" baseline and lag structure. They're a legitimate and important different kind of evidence — but they shouldn't be quoted as if they were certified death counts, in either direction.
  • Migration data is coarse and short. County-level "high flood risk" isn't the same as an individual home's flood zone, and a five-year reversal (2024–25) could itself reverse again — we don't yet know if it's a trend or a blip.
  • Eighty-five years is not that many decades. With only nine data points, one or two outlier storms (a Katrina, a Maria) can swing an entire decade's average — which is exactly why we leaned on per-capita rates and multiple sources rather than any single number.
  • This piece is about deaths, not dollars. Dollar damages are a distinct — and, per the IPCC finding above, an exposure-driven — story that deserves its own treatment, coming later.

Conclusion: fix the exposure, whatever you believe about the cause

Climate change is a real, serious problem, and reducing emissions matters. But almost nothing in this data argues for waiting on that fight before acting on disaster risk — because the actual lifesaving playbook is stuff we should have been doing regardless.

Burke et al.'s own paper makes this point more sharply than we could: after systematically reviewing the intervention literature, they find that many climate-branded policies lack good evidence, while "some of the most beneficial interventions for reducing the health impacts of cold or heat have little explicit to do with climate" at all. Warning systems, building codes, air conditioning access, and health-system capacity are not climate policy — they're just competent disaster preparedness and public health, the same category of investment that cut storm deaths by 65% per capita since the 1940s, long before "climate adaptation" was a budget line. Nordhaus's finding points the same direction: relocating capital as it depreciates anyway is cheap specifically because it piggybacks on ordinary maintenance and construction cycles, not because it depends on any particular climate forecast being right.

That reframes the debate usefully. The question isn't "how much warming will there be, so we know how much to spend." It's: warning systems, resilient building codes, AC access for the elderly and poor, and sensible floodplain and coastal land-use policy pay for themselves today, against the hazards we already have — and they pay again, for free, against whatever additional intensity climate change adds on top. Skip them while waiting for climate certainty, and you're not being prudent; you're leaving the cheapest, best-evidenced part of the problem unsolved.

A coda on scale

Charging a phone for a year costs about a dollar and does about a dollar of carbon damage — genuinely negligible for any one person. Multiply it by eight billion people and it's a few billion dollars a year, roughly a small country's GDP. Nothing about the phone changed; the denominator did.

That's the thread running through this whole story. A death toll, a dollar loss, a migration number — none of them mean anything until you ask "compared to what, and counted how?" The tools of economics don't just price disasters. They tell you which risks you're actually choosing to ignore, and which ones just look small because you're counting them the wrong way.
